Welcome to TOPIK Vocabulary Decoder

If you've reached B1 or beyond in Korean and TOPIK II reading still feels like a wall of almost-familiar words, this workbook was built for you.

Key Concept
Why this workbook doesn't ask you to memorize word lists
Vocabulary at this level can't be learned one word at a time — there are too many words, and the exam introduces new ones in almost every passage. Instead, this workbook teaches you the Hanja roots behind thousands of Korean words, so you can recognize a root and make a strong, reliable inference about a word you've never seen before.
Read more →
+

Three things to know before you begin:

  1. This workbook teaches recognition, not memorization. You're being asked to notice patterns — to see the familiar element inside an unfamiliar word and use it to infer meaning. This is closer to how fluent readers actually process text.
  2. You do not need to know Chinese or Japanese. Hanja are the Chinese-origin characters that underpin a large portion of Korean vocabulary. You need to recognize their Korean readings and meanings — everything here is taught through Korean.
  3. The exercises use words you haven't studied on purpose. From Part 2 onwards, some exercises contain words that haven't appeared in the chapter tables. TOPIK II does the same — the exercises train you to infer, not to recall what you've memorized.

Chapter 1 — What Is Hanja?

Korean is written entirely in Hangul — but the vocabulary inside it is not entirely Korean in origin. A significant portion, called Sino-Korean, is built from Chinese-origin roots called Hanja (한자, 漢字).

Key Concept
The three layers of Korean vocabulary
Korean vocabulary comes from three distinct sources. Knowing where a word comes from tells you how it behaves — and which words are most likely to appear in formal, exam-level contexts.
Read more →
+
35%
Native Korean
Deep Korean roots — physical, sensory, emotional. Common in everyday speech. 하늘, 사람, 밥
~60%
Sino-Korean
Built from Hanja roots. Dominant in formal, academic, written Korean — the focus of this workbook. 학교, 경제, 사회
~5%
Foreign Loanwords
Borrowed from English and other languages — technology, food, pop culture. 커피, 컴퓨터, 버스

The ~60% Sino-Korean figure rises even higher in formal writing and TOPIK II reading passages — this is the vocabulary territory where your exam score is largely decided.

Key Concept
Anatomy of a Hanja-based word
Think of Hanja roots the way you might think of Latin or Greek roots in English — export, import, transport all share the root "port." Hanja roots work the same way, except in Korean almost every formal word is built this way.
Read more →
+

學 — Korean reading 학 (hak) — core meaning: study, learn, scholarship, academic. Always written in Hangul today as 학; 學 is the origin behind the sound.

Four ways 학 combines with other roots:

학+교=학교school — the place of teaching and learning
학+생=학생student — one who lives by studying
입+학=입학school admission — entering a place of study
유+학=유학study abroad — remaining elsewhere to study

Notice 학 appears at the beginning (학교, 학생) or the end (입학, 유학) — the root carries the same meaning in both positions. That flexibility is what makes root recognition powerful.

Chapter 4 — TOPIK II Hanja Strategy

Hanja root knowledge doesn't eliminate uncertainty in TOPIK II reading. It gives you a systematic way to work through it — quickly, calmly, and with enough accuracy to keep your score moving forward.

Key Concept
Understanding the TOPIK II reading environment
Before applying strategy, it helps to understand exactly what kind of vocabulary challenge TOPIK II reading presents.
Text types
Formal & academic — articles, opinion essays, editorials, research summaries, all heavy with Sino-Korean vocabulary.
Vocabulary density
High unfamiliarity rate — even strong learners meet 10–20% unfamiliar words in higher-level passages.
Time pressure
No dictionary access. Every unfamiliar word must be handled with what you already know, or reasoned through.
This profile is exactly why Hanja root recognition is a strategic asset — it converts a hard stop into a workable inference.
Key Concept
The four-step reading strategy
When you meet an unfamiliar word in a TOPIK II reading passage, apply these four steps in order.
Read more →
+
1
Look for roots you recognizeEven one familiar root is useful. If you see 경 in an unfamiliar word, you're in the territory of management, economy, experience, or boundary — depending on what surrounds it.
2
Use the sentence contextContext almost always narrows the meaning of an ambiguous root. Read the full sentence before committing to an interpretation of any single word.
3
Make your best inference and move onYour inference doesn't need to be perfect — it needs to be good enough to understand the sentence. Spending too long on one word costs more than an imperfect reading of it.
4
Check against the questionMany TOPIK II questions don't require every word — only the main argument, a specific detail, or the author's purpose.
Key Concept
Context clues in action
Working through an unfamiliar word: 현대 사회에서 지속가능성은 기업 경영의 핵심 가치가 되었다.
Read more →
+
1
You don't know 지속가능성 as a whole word. You break it down: 지속 (continue/sustain) + 가능 (possible/ability) + 성 (-ness, a common suffix).
2
You read: sustainability-ness → sustainability. Context confirms it: 기업 경영 (corporate management) + 핵심 가치 (core value).
3
Full meaning: "In modern society, sustainability has become a core value of corporate management." You didn't need to have studied 지속가능성 — you reasoned to it.

Key Concept
Practical exam day guidance
Do not freeze on unfamiliar words — apply the four-step strategy, make your inference, and move forward.
Read more →
+
  • Do not freeze on unfamiliar words. Time spent agonizing over one word costs comprehension of the whole passage.
  • Trust your root knowledge. If you recognize a root, that recognition is information — use it. An inference based on roots is almost always more accurate than a blind guess.

Pattern Spotlight

Pattern Spotlight

환 (surround/ring) + 경 (condition/boundary) = 환경 (the conditions surrounding us = environment). The same logic applies to 순환 (循環) — 순 (follow/circulate) + 환 (ring) = circulation. When you see 환 in an environment word, think: something encircling, surrounding, or returning.

환경 — environment · 순환 — circulation · 환원 — restoration · 환경오염 — pollution

Chapter 21 — Common Prefixes

Prefixes are the most powerful single tool in Hanja-based vocabulary inference. When you can read the first element of an unfamiliar compound, you already know the direction of its meaning — before you've even decoded the rest of the word. This chapter covers the twelve prefixes that appear most often in TOPIK II reading passages.

Key Concept
Distinguishing 불 · 비 · 무 — three negation prefixes
불 = not (state negation) · 비 = non- (category exclusion) · 무 = without / zero (complete absence). 불안 = unstable state. 비공식 = not in the official category. 무관심 = literally no interest at all.
Prefix 1 · 불 / 부 — Negation
不 (불·부) — Not / Un- / In- / Dis-
The single most productive prefix in Korean Sino-vocabulary. It negates almost any noun or adjective root. If you see 불 or 부 at the start of a word you don't know, your first inference is always: the opposite of whatever follows.
불평등불법불안불가능 불변불만부족부적절
💡 Pattern

불/부 + any positive word = the opposite. 가능 (possible) → 불가능 (impossible). You don't need to memorize these separately — derive them on demand.

Prefix 2 · 비 — Non- / Anti- / Without
非 (비) — Non- / Not / Without / Anti-
Signals the absence or negation of a quality — more formal and academic than 불/부. It tends to appear in policy and academic writing rather than everyday speech.

Prefix 3 · 무 — Without / -less / Absence

再 (재) — Re- / Again: 재활용 (recycling), 재생 (regeneration), 재검토 (re-examination), 재건 (reconstruction). The Korean equivalent of English re-.

超 (초) — Super- / Ultra- / Beyond: 초고령화 (super-ageing society), 초저출산 (ultra-low birth rate), 초국가적 (transnational), 초과 (excess).

前 (전) — Pre- / Before / Prior: 전제 (precondition), 사전에 (in advance), 전통 (tradition), 전환 (shift/transition). Contrast with 後 (after).

大 (대) — Large / Major / Grand: 대규모 (large-scale), 대중 (the masses), 대응 (response), 대책 (countermeasure). Contrast with 小 (small/minor).

Chapter 22 — Common Suffixes

If prefixes change the direction of a word, suffixes determine its shape — what kind of thing it is. The same root can become a person (자), a quality (성), an ability (력), or a process (화) depending on the suffix attached. This is the final chapter of the workbook.

Group 1 · Person Suffixes
Turn a root into a person or role

-자 (者)person who does X / -er / -ist연구자 · 학자 · 소비자 · 노동자
-인 (人)person / member of group외국인 · 성인 · 노인 · 개인
-가 (家)expert / specialist / professional전문가 · 음악가 · 정치가 · 작가
인 identifies a person by category or origin; 가 implies professional or artistic mastery; 자 is the most general "person who does."
